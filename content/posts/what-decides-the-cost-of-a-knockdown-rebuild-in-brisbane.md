# What decides the cost of a knockdown rebuild in Brisbane?

**The short answer.** Nobody can give you an honest price for a knockdown rebuild from a website,
and the online calculators are pricing a different block from yours. Six things actually decide
your number: what has to come out of the old house before it can be demolished, what the ground
underneath does, how much house you're building and to what finish, whether your street sits under
Brisbane's character rules, what a builder has quietly included or left out of the figure they've
given you, and where your family lives while it happens. Two houses on the same street, same size,
same year built, can land a long way apart on all six. So the useful move isn't hunting for a
number online. It's working out which of the six apply to your block — and most of them can be
checked before you spend anything.

Here they are, one at a time.

## Why isn't there a price on this page?

Because we'd be making it up, and you'd find out we'd made it up somewhere around the third month.

A knockdown rebuild isn't a product with a price. It's demolition, then earthworks, then a house,
on a block nobody has looked at yet. The demolition depends on what's in the old house. The
earthworks depend on the dirt. The house depends on how big it is and how it's finished. Change
the block and you change three of those at once.

The cost calculators you'll find get around this by quietly assuming an easy block, a simple house
and a standard finish. If that's your situation, great. If your driveway is too tight for a truck,
or the ground is reactive clay, or the house has asbestos sheeting under the paint, the calculator
never knew and the number was never yours.

What we can do is be straight about what moves it.

## What has to come out before the house comes down?

Demolition sounds like the simple part. It's the part with the most unknowns in it.

**Asbestos is the big one.** Asbestos was used in Australian building products right up until 1990,
and it wasn't fully banned until 2003. If your house was built or renovated before then, it may
well contain it — commonly in wall sheeting, eaves, bathroom and laundry linings, old vinyl floors
and fencing. On a Bayside house from the fifties, sixties or seventies, assume it's there until
someone qualified says it isn't.

That's not a reason to panic, and it isn't a reason to leave the house standing. It's a reason the
demolition figure moves. Asbestos removal before a demolition is licensed work in Queensland. The
removalist has to prepare an asbestos removal control plan, and licensed removal work and certain
demolition work have to be notified to Workplace Health and Safety Queensland before anyone starts.
That's people, paperwork and disposal, and it's priced on how much there is.

**Then there's everything else in and under the house.** Services have to be disconnected properly
before anything comes down: power, water, sewer, gas, sometimes telecommunications. Old sheds,
slabs, tanks and pool shells have to go somewhere. And what's under the house only becomes visible
once the house isn't over it.

## What does the ground actually do?

The block decides more than the house does, and it decides it before a wall goes up.

**Soil.** Australian footings are designed to a standard called AS 2870, which sorts sites into
classes from A, which is stable sand or rock, through S, M, H1, H2 and E, up to P for a problem
site. The classes measure how much the ground swells and shrinks as it gets wet and dries out.
Reactive clay is common across eastern Australia, including plenty of Brisbane. A more reactive
site needs more engineering in the footings, and that's real money that never shows up in the
finished house. You find out from a soil test, not from looking at it.

**Slope.** A flat block and a sloping block are two different jobs. Slope means cut and fill,
retaining, sometimes a different design altogether. It's also the thing a good design can work
with instead of fighting — a house that steps down a slope can cost less than a house that
flattens it.

**Access.** How does a truck get in? What's the driveway like, what's overhead, how tight is the
street? A block a machine can't easily reach is a block where everything takes longer, and time is
what you're paying for.

**What's already running through it.** Sewer, stormwater and easements decide where a house can
physically go. Trees do too, and some are protected. None of this is exotic — it's just the sort of
thing that has to be known before a design is drawn rather than after.

## Does the character overlay change the cost?

If your house was built in 1946 or earlier, and your street is in Brisbane City Council's
Traditional building character overlay, then yes — because demolition needs a development
application and council decides.

That adds time and it adds the cost of the application. It also adds a real possibility that the
answer is no, in which case the money is better spent on a renovation and you want to know that
early rather than late.

The overlay is mapped street by street, so one side of a road can be in it and the other side not.

## How much house are you building?

This is the lever you control, and it's the biggest one.

Floor area is the obvious part. The less obvious parts cost just as much:

- **Storeys.** Going up costs more per square metre than going out, and going out costs you block.
- **The shape of the roof and the outline of the house.** Every corner, every change in roof plane
  and every step in the floor plan is more framing, more flashing, more labour. A simple, well
  proportioned house is not a cheap house — it's a house that spent its money on the rooms.
- **Wet areas.** Bathrooms and kitchens carry the most trades per square metre in the building. A
  fourth bathroom costs a lot more than the same floor area of bedroom.
- **How much of it is under roof but not inside.** Garages, alfrescos and porches are cheaper per
  square metre than living space, but they aren't free, and they're where a "total area" figure
  can quietly flatter a comparison.

## What level of finish?

Two identical houses can be a long way apart on this alone, and it's the part families
underestimate most, because it's the part you choose late, one item at a time, when you're tired.

Tapware, tiles, benchtops, joinery, flooring, windows, the front door, the stairs. None of it
changes the size of the house. All of it changes the number. The honest way to handle it is to
decide early roughly where you want to sit, and have it written into the contract as a real
allowance rather than a placeholder that gets revisited.

## What's in the figure — and what isn't?

This is where two quotes stop being comparable, and it's worth more attention than most families
give it.

Things that are part of a knockdown rebuild whether or not they're in the number you've been shown:

- **Demolition, and the asbestos work inside it.**
- **The soil test and the engineering that follows from it.**
- **Approvals** — building approval always, and a development application if the overlay applies.
- **Home warranty insurance.** In Queensland, most residential building work over $3,300 including
  materials, labour and GST has to be covered under the Queensland Home Warranty Scheme. The
  builder collects the premium and pays it to the QBCC, and it's part of the contract price.
- **Getting services back on** — power, water, sewer, stormwater connections to the new house.
- **Driveway, paths, fencing, letterbox, clothesline, landscaping.** The cheapest thing to leave
  out of a quote and the most obvious thing to be missing when you move in.
- **Where you live in the meantime** (below).

When you're comparing two builders, don't compare the two numbers. Compare the two lists. A figure
that's twenty thousand lower because four of those items aren't in it isn't lower.

## Where does the family live, and for how long?

For a full knockdown rebuild, not in the house — there isn't one.

Most Bayside families rent nearby so the kids stay in the same school, and plan the build around
the school year. It's rent, plus moving twice, plus storage, and it runs for the whole build. It is
routinely the largest cost that isn't in anybody's quote, because it isn't the builder's to quote.

Ask about it in the first conversation, not the fifth. Raises, extensions and renovations are a
different story — some of those can be worked around a family staying put, and a builder who's
walked your house should tell you honestly which one yours is.

## So how do you actually find out what yours costs?

Someone looks at it. That's the whole answer.

Before that, three things are worth doing yourself, and none of them cost anything:

1. **Check whether your street is in the character overlay** — five minutes on council's mapping.
2. **Find out roughly when the house was built.** It sets the asbestos question and the overlay
   question at the same time.
3. **Write down what your family actually needs from the next house.** Not styles. Rooms, and who's
   in them, in five years.

Then get the block walked. Ours is free and there's no obligation, and Nathan will tell you if the
answer is that you shouldn't rebuild.

## Key takeaways

- **There is no honest online price.** Any calculator is pricing an assumed block, an assumed
  house and an assumed finish.
- **Demolition is the least predictable part**, and asbestos in anything built before 1990 is the
  usual reason.
- **The ground decides a lot before the house starts** — soil class, slope, access and services.
- **A pre-1947 house in the character overlay** needs a development application to come down, which
  is time, cost, and a real chance of no.
- **Finish level moves the number as much as floor area**, and it's chosen late when you're tired.
- **Compare inclusion lists, not totals.** Approvals, warranty insurance, connections, driveway
  and landscaping are the usual omissions.
- **Rent during the build is usually the biggest cost nobody quoted**, because it isn't the
  builder's to quote.

## Common questions

**Is a knockdown rebuild cheaper than renovating?**
Sometimes. A renovation on an older Bayside house can uncover wiring, plumbing and sub-floor all at
the end of their lives, and fixing those inside an existing house is more fiddly than building new.
The honest answer depends on your house, which is why it's the first thing we look at.

**Can you give me a rough per-square-metre figure?**
Not one you should trust. Per-square-metre rates hide the two things that vary most — what the
ground does and what the finish is — and they always exclude the demolition, which is the part
your block will differ on most.

**Does the block being in Wynnum or Manly change the cost?**
Only through the block itself: the age of the house, the soil, the slope, the access, the overlay.
Suburb doesn't set a price. The land does.

**When will I get a real number?**
After someone has stood on the block and been through the house. Anything before that is a guess
dressed up, and guesses are where the horror stories start.

**What's the cheapest thing I can do to keep the cost down?**
Keep the shape of the house simple and decide the finish level early. Those two, before anything
else.
