# How to check if your house is in the character overlay

**The short answer.** Two free checks and about fifteen minutes will tell you where you stand.
The first is Brisbane City Council's City Plan online mapping: type in your address, turn on the
overlays, and it will tell you whether your property sits in the **Traditional building character
overlay**. The second is the year your house was built, which the mapping won't always tell you —
for that you want Brisbane City Archives, who hold building records going back to 1904. You need
both answers, because the rules only bite where a pre-1947 house and the overlay meet. A 1960s
house inside the overlay is a different situation from a 1930s house inside it, and a 1930s house
outside the overlay is different again. Neither check costs anything, and you can do both before
you talk to anybody.

## Check one: is your property in the overlay?

Council publishes the whole planning scheme, maps included, in a tool called **City Plan online**.

1. Go to **cityplan.brisbane.qld.gov.au/eplan** and type your address into the search.
2. Open the map for your property.
3. In the **Maps Tools** panel, turn on the **Overlays** layers. The one you're looking for is
   **Traditional building character**.
4. Generate the **property report**. It's a short summary of the City Plan requirements that apply
   to your site, and it lists the overlays affecting your lot in one place.

Two things people get wrong here.

**It's mapped street by street, not suburb by suburb.** One side of a road can be in the overlay
and the other side not. What the neighbours did tells you nothing reliable about your block.

**"In the overlay" is not the same as "can't be touched."** The overlay is about the look of the
street. It's what makes demolition of a pre-1947 house something council decides on rather than
something you simply do. The rules and the limited situations where a pre-1947 house can still
come down are set out here: **[Can we knock down our house? Brisbane's rules in plain
words](/blog/can-we-knock-down-our-house/)**

If you'd rather ask a person, council's general number is **07 3403 8888**.

## Check two: what year did your house go up?

This is the question most families can't answer about their own home, and it matters more than the
overlay does. The line is 1946. Built in 1946 or earlier, and the character rules are in play if
your street is in the overlay. Built after, and they almost certainly aren't.

Council has a practice note on exactly this — *determining the construction date of a building* —
because it comes up constantly. The records themselves sit with **Brisbane City Archives**:

- **Building Application Registers** run from **March 1904 to October 1945**, arranged by year and
  indexed by street and by owner's name.
- **From November 1945**, every building application went onto an individual property card. Those
  cards record new houses as well as extensions and alterations, and they're searchable.

⚠️ **The records have holes.** There are no surviving registers for **September 1934 to January
1936** or **April to December 1937**, and for **1939 and 1941** only the index survives. If your
house went up in one of those windows, absence of a record isn't evidence the house isn't there —
it's evidence the paperwork didn't survive. That's worth knowing before you conclude anything.

The Archives are at Moorooka and you can ring them on **07 3403 1711**.

Other things that help date a house: the title, historical aerial photography, and the house
itself. Stumps, VJ walls, casement windows and a particular kind of roof line tell a builder a
lot in about thirty seconds, which is one reason a walk-through is worth more than a phone call.

## What do you do with the two answers?

Four combinations, and they lead different places.

| Your house | Your street | Where that leaves you |
|---|---|---|
| Built after 1946 | Not in the overlay | The character rules aren't your issue. Normal approvals still apply |
| Built after 1946 | In the overlay | Usually fine for the house itself, but the overlay still shapes what a new house looks like from the street |
| Built 1946 or earlier | Not in the overlay | The pre-1947 protections are tied to the overlay, so this is a much simpler conversation than people expect |
| Built 1946 or earlier | In the overlay | Demolition needs a development application and council decides. Not automatically no. Not automatically yes |

If you land in the bottom row, don't assume the answer is no and don't assume it's yes. It's a
case to be made, with evidence, and it's worth getting a builder and a town planner to look at it
before you either give up on the block or spend money as though approval is a formality.

And if the house was built **before 1911**, council's position is that it's kept. That's the one
row where the answer is usually the end of the conversation for a full knockdown.

## Common questions

**Does the overlay stop me renovating?**
No. It shapes what work on a pre-1947 house looks like from the street, and it's why some changes
need approval that you'd assume wouldn't. Renovating inside the overlay happens all the time.

**Is the map ever wrong?**
The map is council's, and it changes from time to time. It's the source of truth on the day you
read it, which is exactly why you check it yourself rather than relying on what someone told you
about the street three years ago.

**My neighbour knocked theirs down. Doesn't that settle it?**
No. The overlay is mapped street by street, their house may have been a different year, and their
application may have been decided on things specific to their house. It's a good sign, not an
answer.
