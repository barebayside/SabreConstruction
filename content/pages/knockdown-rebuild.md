# Knock the old house down. Stay on the street.

You picked the street. The school, the walk to the water, the neighbours who wave. The house is
the part that stopped working.

A knockdown rebuild is the third answer, and most families have never seriously looked at it.
You take the old house away and build a new one on the same block. Same address, same school
run, same neighbours. A house built around your family instead of around a family from 1968.

## What is a knockdown rebuild?

A knockdown rebuild means demolishing the house you own and building a new one on the same land.
You keep the block, the address and the suburb. You don't pay stamp duty on a new purchase, you
don't move the kids, and you don't spend two years looking for something that doesn't exist.

It suits a family whose block is good and whose house is finished. That is most of the older
Bayside: a solid, flat or gently sloping block near the water or a school, holding a house with
sub-floor, wiring and plumbing at the end of their lives. Renovating buys you a better version of
the same house. Rebuilding gets you the house you'd have chosen, on the land you already chose.

The main things that decide whether it is realistic on your block are the age of the house,
because Brisbane protects the look of some older streets, and what the block itself will take.
Both are checkable before you spend anything.

## Can we even knock it down?

That is the first question, and most families can't answer it about their own house.

If your house was built in 1946 or earlier, and your street sits in Brisbane City Council's
**Traditional building character overlay**, demolition needs a development application and council
decides. It isn't automatically no. It isn't automatically yes either. If it was built after 1946,
the character rules almost certainly don't stop you. If it was built before 1911, council's
position is that the house is kept.

The overlay is mapped street by street. One side of a road can be in it and the other side not,
which is why guessing from what the neighbours did is a bad idea.

It takes about five minutes to check your own address, and we've written the plain-words version
here: **[Can we knock down our house?](/blog/can-we-knock-down-our-house/)**

Or answer two questions on the contact page and we'll check it with you.

## Where does the family live while it's built?

For a full knockdown rebuild, not in the house — there isn't one. Most Bayside families rent
nearby so the kids stay in the same school, and they plan the build around the school year.

It's the cost people forget, so it's one of the first things Nathan raises on the walk-through,
before anyone signs anything. Raises, extensions and renovations are different, and some of those
can be worked around a family staying put. He'll tell you honestly which yours is.

## Common questions

**Do we need plans or a design before we ring you?**
No. Most families start with Nathan walking the house. The design comes after, drawn around what
he sees and what you tell him.

**Is a knockdown rebuild cheaper than renovating?**
Sometimes, and not always. A renovation that opens a wall and finds old wiring, old plumbing and a
sub-floor at the end of its life can end up costing more than the new house would have. The honest
answer needs someone to look at your house.

**Can we keep part of the old house?**
Usually very little of it is worth keeping, but that's a real question with a real answer, and it's
worth asking on the walk-through rather than assuming either way.

**What if the answer is that we shouldn't rebuild?**
Then we'll say so. A block that can't take it, or a house that's better renovated, is a better
conversation to have in your kitchen than three meetings in.
